In the Gardens of Exegesis: The Tafsīr of Sheikh Ibrāhīm Niasse

Amadu Kunateh

PhD Candidate, Committee on the Study of Religion

Harvard University

Sheikh Ibrāhīm Niasse and the West African Scholarly Tradition

Shaykh Ibrāhīm Niasse (d. 1975), the twentieth-century Senegalese scholar and leader of the Fayḍa branch of the Tijāniyya ṭarīqa, is regarded by his followers as the Ṣāḥib al-Fayḍa, the Bringer of the Evental Flood of Gnosis.[1] A prolific author and teacher, he emerged from an intellectual formation shaped entirely by the West African scholarly tradition. He studied exclusively in the region, primarily under the guidance of his father, al-Ḥājj ʿAbd Allāh Niasse (d. 1922), whose own formation placed him within a lineage of West African scholars who had, from the fifteenth century onward, contributed substantially to Islamic theology, logic, jurisprudence, and spirituality.[2] Figures such as Aḥmad ibn Aqīt al-Timbukti, al-Kashnawī al-Fulānī, ʿUthmān dan Fodio, and Aḥmadu Bamba left an enduring legacy of rational sciences and theological works that shaped the scholarly environment in which Niasse was educated.

This formation immersed Niasse in the theological debates and intellectual currents of West Africa, allowing him to engage deeply with the region's distinctive approaches to Islamic scholarship. A compelling anecdote underscores his erudition: he reportedly memorized Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī's Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb in its entirety, one of the most philosophically sophisticated Quranic commentaries in the classical tradition. This command of the tradition positions him as a significant interlocutor within the broader landscape of Islamic thought.

The compiler of the Arabic edition, Muḥammad ibn Shaykh ʿAbd Allāh, characterises the Sheikh's relationship to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, as 'the vertebral column of the fruits of his ceaseless interaction with the world and his constant articulation of his biography,' a relationship, he adds, 'that no word can express and no speech can convey.'[4] In his gathered collections of Muhammadan praise poetry there are approximately six thousand verses, and in every verse, as the compiler writes, 'you read and hear depth and abundance, and you feel the breadth of the praiser and the greatness of the praised.'

The scope of Niasse's scholarly mission extended across the inhabited world. The compiler records that he traversed regions 'by land and sea and air for the call to God,' visiting Paris, Cairo, Beirut, Tehran, India, Hong Kong, and China.[4] Among the fruits of this effort, the compiler writes, was that 'there entered into Islam at his hands, from the various peoples of the inhabited world, what the means of counting and enumeration failed to enumerate.' His da'wa was accompanied by a sustained intellectual engagement with the challenges of his age. His opposition to colonialism and its cultural consequences was, in the compiler's words, 'at the utmost clarity and courage and openness.' At a conference of the Muslim World League, when a speaker proposed that slavery had no basis in Islamic law, Niasse opposed him directly with the texts of the Quran, the practice of the Messenger, and the practice of the Companions, insisting that the integrity of the tradition required fidelity to its established rulings.

Tafsīr was a fitting medium for Niasse's intellectual contributions. His scholarly audience, referred to as al-muntahūn (the accomplished), was deeply trained in the Islamic sciences. These were sessions in which, as the compiler notes, scholars initiated the gathering and laypeople joined, demonstrating the accessibility and dynamism of his teachings.[4]

The Quran as Niasse's Miracle and Scholarly Medium

The compiler of the Arabic edition offers a characterisation of Niasse's relationship to the Quran that is central to understanding this work. Shaykh Ibrāhīm (r.a.) mentions that the character traits of all the prophets are gathered in Allah's Messenger (ṣ.a.w.s.), as are all of their prophetic miracles, and his miracle is the Quran, which endures until the end of time: 'Verily, We have sent down the Reminder, and We are its Preservers' (Q. 15:9); 'Its gathering and recitation is incumbent upon Us; and when We recite it, follow its recitation. Then its elucidation is likewise incumbent upon Us' (Q. 75:17-19).[4]

Shaykh Ibrāhīm further declares in verse: 'Make the preservation of Your Noble Book my saintly miracle until the great meeting with You.' Just as the Noble Quran constitutes the prophetic miracle of Allah's Messenger, so too does it function as a saintly miracle (karāma) for Shaykh Ibrāhīm, manifested in the degree to which he preserved it, expounded it, comprehended it, and guided others by it.[4] It is well attested that he acted upon it continuously and would complete a full recitation three times each week.

Niasse's public proclamation of his distinctive spiritual role coincided with his delivery of this Quranic commentary during the month of Ramadan (c. 1929). The history of the Fayḍa movement and Niasse's own self-identification are therefore inseparable from this text. It was tafsīr that first offered him the opportunity to demonstrate and substantiate his claim, for, as the well-known maxim states, 'knowledge is the leader' (al-ʿilm huwa l-imām).

Quranic exegesis has long been regarded as the pinnacle of Islamic scholarship, integrating multiple disciplines. As al-Suyūṭī states in al-Itqān, a mufassir must master at least fifteen sciences.[5] Niasse's commentary exemplifies this tradition, engaging with topics from theology to metaphysics, from jurisprudence to the subtleties of Arabic grammar. This commentary aligns with Niasse's broader vision of tajdīd (renewal), which he describes as a restoration of authentic faith in response to theological confusion and sectarian disputes.[7]

Fī Riyāḍ al-Tafsīr: Origins, Character, and Scope

Fī Riyāḍ al-Tafsīr li-l-Qurʾān al-Karīm is a ten-volume monumental work composed for Niasse's community and a wider readership.[1] The title, In the Gardens of Quranic Commentary, is characteristic of his self-presentation: the image of the garden evokes abundance, cultivation, and patient growth. The work originated as an oral commentary delivered in the setting of his teaching circle. The tafsīr that has reached us was delivered in Arabic in 1383 AH (c. 1963-64). It was recorded on cassette tapes and subsequently transcribed by the compiler, Muḥammad ibn Shaykh ʿAbd Allāh al-Tijānī al-Ibrāhīmī, who subjected the text to a rigorous process of hadith verification and scholarly annotation.

The compiler acknowledges the challenges of transcribing oral speech: the density of pronominal reference, the rapid movement of inflection, and the inevitable gap between spoken expression and written text. He undertook the work, as he writes, out of concern that the recordings, scattered among the khalīfas and companions of the Sheikh, might be lost.[4] The hadith apparatus alone records more than six thousand citations, traced wherever possible to their canonical sources across the ṣaḥīḥayn, the Sunan collections, and the wider hadith literature.

The work was authorised for publication by the Niasse family, with unrestricted permission granted for its writing, verification, printing, and dissemination. The scholarly foundation of the Arabic text is the compiler's labour of more than a decade, for which this edition is deeply indebted.

The commentary engages the full range of Islamic scholarly disciplines, moving from grammatical analysis of Quranic syntax to discussions of divine attributes, and from legal rulings to the direct address of the spiritual community. Niasse draws on the great works of the tafsīr tradition, including the Rūḥ al-bayān of Ismāʿīl Ḥaqqī al-Burūsawī, the al-Jāmiʿ of al-Qurṭubī, and the Ḥāshiya of al-Ṣāwī, alongside Tafsīr al-Jalālayn, while consistently returning to the oral, pedagogical, and spiritually formative dimensions of Quranic engagement.[8] West Africa has its own tafsīr tradition of considerable antiquity: the oldest known locally composed Arabic tafsīr dates to the first half of the eighteenth century, and the tradition deepened substantially through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.[9] Fī Riyāḍ al-Tafsīr stands as the most comprehensive and intellectually ambitious work in this tradition.

This Edition: Structure, Method, and Translation Approach

The present edition presents the Arabic text of Fī Riyāḍ al-Tafsīr in the Warsh ʿan Nāfiʿ rasm, the orthographic standard of North and West Africa in which the tafsīr was delivered, with an English translation on facing pages. The edition is structured in seven volumes, each corresponding to one of the seven manzils, the weekly Quranic recitation cycle that Niasse himself practised and celebrated in verse. He enumerated the opening surah of each manzil in verse and invoked two of the Quran's names, al-Furqān (the Criterion) and al-Jamʿ (the Joining):[6]

Al-Fātiḥah, al-Māʾida, Yūnus, al-Isrāʾ, al-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Ṣāffāt, Qāf, thus it is clarified. He who joins the Criterion with the Joining completes a full recitation of the Criterion.

This structure reflects fidelity to the Sheikh's own relationship to the Quran. Quranic audio is presented per verse using the recitation of Maḥmūd Khalīl al-Ḥuṣarī (1917-1980) in the Warsh riwāya.

The translation proceeds from a commitment to scholarly precision and readability. The register is formal academic prose that preserves the oral cadences of the original, its directness, its rhythmic repetitions, and its transitions between legal analysis and spiritual address. Technical terms are retained in Arabic on first occurrence, with English equivalents in parentheses, and thereafter used in transliteration. The transliteration follows IJMES conventions throughout.

Three translation choices warrant explicit statement. First, the Basmala is rendered 'By Allah's Name, The Entirely Merciful, The Especially Merciful,' preserving the theological distinction between al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm that Niasse himself elaborates at length. Second, the term ʿabd is translated as 'slave' throughout, as the more precise rendering of the term's theological weight, the relationship of complete ontological submission that ʿabd carries in Niasse's usage. Third, the phrase al-ḥamdu lillāh is rendered as two words, 'al-ḥamdu lillāh (The Praise is for God),' preserving both the Arabic formula and its syntactic structure.

The footnote apparatus throughout this edition preserves all of Niasse's original scholarly citations, to the hadith collections, to al-Suyūṭī's al-Itqān, to al-Ghazālī, to Ibn ʿArabī, traced to their canonical sources. Translator's notes are added only where necessary and are clearly distinguished from Niasse's own footnotes.

فَحَتَّى الأَدْنَى مِنْ بَنِيَّ يَفْنَى

"And I thank my Lord that my secret is not sterile or barren, for even the least adequate of my followers will achieve annihilation."

أُسَابِقُهُم مَا عِشتُ فِيكَ وَإِن أَمُتْ يُسَاجِلُهُم فَرعِي وَفِيكَ يُسَابِقُ
