"I seek refuge in Allah from Satan the accursed." Anyone who enters upon a science must know its definition and its principles. The definition of this science is: the knowledge by which the meanings of the speech of Allah are known. Its name is the science of tafsīr.

Tafsīr and taʾwīl are not one and the same in meaning. Tafsīr means the explanation of the speech of Allah, Blessed and Exalted, from the perspective of what has been transmitted (al-manqūl). Taʾwīl means confirming what the text can sustain of meaning from the perspective of reason (al-ʿaql). Transmitted reports are indispensable in tafsīr, for the Qurʾān says to the Messenger, may Allah bless him and grant him peace: "so that you might make clear to the people what has been sent down to them" (Q. 16:44). For this reason tafsīr relies upon what the Messenger explained. When no transmitted report from the Messenger is found, tafsīr by rational inference is accepted, because Allah, Blessed and Exalted, says: "an Arabic Qurʾān, so that perhaps you might reason" (Q. 43:3). This verse has given us the right to exercise reason in order to understand the meanings of the speech of Allah, Blessed and Exalted.

ʿAlī, may Allah honor his face, was asked: "Do you possess a book that the Messenger of Allah singled out for the People of the House?" He replied: "No, only the Book of Allah, or an understanding of it that Allah grants to a Muslim man." And ʿAlī, may Allah honor his face, said: "The Qurʾān is supple, possessed of many aspects; accept its aspects." Whoever holds fast to something that has an aspect in the Qurʾān, let none dispute it with him.¹

Both tafsīr and taʾwīl must rest upon transmission or reason. The Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, forbade interpreting the Qurʾān by mere personal opinion. The interpretation of the Qurʾān requires knowledge of Arabic speech, knowledge of the legal rulings, knowledge of rhetoric and literary craft, and a light in the heart of the interpreter.²

This Book was sent down to us by Allah as guidance and light, unlike all other books. Whoever holds fast to it arrives at what he seeks; whoever neglects it goes astray. It is the word of the Lord of all existence, revealed in the language of the Arabs, the most eloquent of all languages. Allah, Blessed and Exalted, says: "Verily We have revealed it as an Arabic Qurʾān so that you might reason" (Q. 12:2). It is reported from the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace: "The superiority of the speech of Allah over all other speech is like the superiority of Allah over His creation." The science of tafsīr is the noblest of all sciences, because the nobility of any science is measured by the nobility of its subject-matter, and the subject of this science is the speech of Allah, Blessed and Exalted. No speech is nobler than the speech of Allah; therefore no science is nobler than the science of tafsīr. Any science that requires the science of tafsīr is itself a subordinate science, for the Qurʾān is the foundation upon which every other Islamic science rests: ḥadīth, fiqh, kalām, and taṣawwuf all return to it for their authority and validity.

The commentator of the Qurʾān occupies the highest station among the scholars, because he speaks about the meaning of the speech of Allah. This station entails a corresponding obligation: he must know the Arabic language in its depth and breadth, and he must possess sound understanding so that he does not carry the speech of Allah to a meaning it cannot bear.

The wisdom in beginning the recitation of the Qurʾān with the words of seeking refuge (al-istiʿādha) is that the reciter is about to engage with the word of Allah, which is the greatest of all speech. Satan desires to distract him from understanding it, to cast doubt into his heart, or to cause him to stumble in its recitation. By saying "I seek refuge in Allah from Satan the accursed," the reciter places himself under the protection of Allah, who drives Satan away from him so that he may receive the divine speech with a pure heart and attentive mind.

Allah, Blessed and Exalted, commands this at Q. 16:98: "When you recite the Qurʾān, seek refuge in Allah from Satan the accursed." The word rajīm (accursed) comes from the root r-j-m, meaning to stone and drive away. Satan is named rajīm because he was cast out and banished from the mercy of Allah.

A student once went repeatedly to a scholar in Khurāsān until he had memorized four thousand ḥadīths from him. He said: "I shall go to the other scholars and debate with them, for I have become learned." He came to a gathering of scholars and began to speak; they refuted him on every point, until he felt ashamed and returned to his teacher. He said: "Master, I memorized four thousand ḥadīths from you, yet the scholars refuted me at every turn." This story illustrates that tafsīr requires not only the memorization of transmitted material but deep understanding, sound reasoning, and God-given insight. A person may memorize the words of scholars and yet remain ignorant of their meanings.

"By Allah’s Name — The Entirely Merciful, The Especially Merciful."

The scholars differed extensively over the basmala and its status: whether it is an independent verse of Sūrat al-Fātiḥa, or a verse revealed at the head of every sūra but not of the Fātiḥa specifically, or a separator between sūras with no independent verse-status of its own. The scholars of Qurʾānic recitation differ: the Medinans, led by Nāfiʿ, hold that it is not a verse of the Fātiḥa; the Kūfans, led by ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī, hold that it is.³

What is established without dispute is that the basmala was revealed as part of the Qurʾān. Its revelation marked the beginning of every sūra, and the Messenger, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, knew the conclusion of one sūra and the beginning of the next by the descent of the basmala. The Prophet never began any affair of consequence without saying it, and the believer is instructed to begin every righteous act with it so as to seek the blessing (baraka) of Allah's name.⁵

The basmala comprises nineteen letters, and the guardians of Hell who attend the Fire are nineteen in number (Q. 74:30). The scholars of tafsīr note the correspondence: whoever takes refuge in the basmala with true sincerity may hope for protection from each of the nineteen.

"I recite in the name of Allah" or "we recite in the name of Allah": a person begins and does not say "I begin" (abtadiʾu), because "beginning" can be understood in a material sense, whereas reciting is a spiritual act from the outset. The bāʾ here is for accompaniment (mulābasa): it indicates that the recitation is performed while being accompanied by, or grounded in, the name of Allah.⁴

The name Allāh is the greatest and most comprehensive of the divine names. It is the name that the Qurʾān ascribes to the Being who possesses all perfection; all the other names (al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm, al-Malik, and the rest) are attributes that qualify it. The name Allāh is not shared by any created being; no child has ever been named Allāh, nor has any idol. It is therefore the most particular and the most sublime of the names of Allah, Blessed and Exalted.

Al-Raḥmān (the Compassionate) and al-Raḥīm (the Merciful) are both derived from the root r-ḥ-m (mercy). Al-Raḥmān denotes an intense, encompassing mercy that extends over all creation in this world, believers and disbelievers alike. Al-Raḥīm denotes a mercy that is reserved for the believers specifically in the hereafter. This is why the Qurʾān says: "And He is ever Merciful (raḥīm) to the believers" (Q. 33:43): the specific mercy of the raḥīm is for the people of faith.

Sūrat al-Fātiḥa is known by many names, each of which indicates an aspect of its greatness. It is called Sūrat al-Ṣalāh (the Sūra of Prayer) because the prayer is not valid without it. It is called al-Ṣalāh (the Prayer) itself, by the name of the act of worship that requires it. It is called Umm al-Qurʾān (the Mother of the Qurʾān) and Umm al-Kitāb (the Mother of the Book), because it contains the essence of the entire Qurʾān and is the source from which its meanings spring. It is called al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī (the Seven Oft-Repeated) because it consists of seven verses and is repeated in every rakʿa of the prayer. It is called al-Kāfiya (the Sufficient) because it suffices in the prayer without any other sūra, whereas no other sūra can substitute for it.⁸

Sūrat al-Fātiḥa is the greatest sūra in the Qurʾān. The Messenger, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, was asked: "Which sūra of the Qurʾān is the greatest?" He replied: "Al-Ḥamdu li-llāhi rabb al-ʿālamīn — it is the Seven Oft-Repeated and the Mighty Qurʾān that was given to me" (Q. 15:87).

In a sacred ḥadīth transmitted by Muslim, Allah, Mighty and Majestic, says: "I have divided the prayer between Myself and My slave in two halves, and My slave shall have what he asks for." When the slave says al-Ḥamdu li-llāhi rabb al-ʿālamīn, Allah says: "My slave has praised Me." When he says al-Raḥmāni l-Raḥīm, Allah says: "My slave has extolled Me." When he says Māliki yawm al-dīn, Allah says: "My slave has glorified Me — and has submitted himself to Me." When he says Iyyāka naʿbudu wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn, Allah says: "This is between Myself and My slave, and My slave shall have what he asks for." When he says Ihdina l-ṣirāṭa l-mustaqīm to the end, Allah says: "This is for My slave, and My slave shall have what he asks for."⁶

This is the greatest honor a slave can receive: that Allah personally responds to each verse of the prayer with words of acceptance and acknowledgment. No other act of worship carries this direct divine dialogue in the same form.⁷

"al-ḥamdu lillāh (The Praise is for God), Lord of all the worlds."

Al-ḥamd (praise) covers every expression of gratitude and commendation that exists. Every act of praise and thanksgiving that any created being has ever offered belongs either to its description or to its consequence. The categories of ḥamd are divided according to whether it pertains to Allah's attributes or to His acts. The attributes of Allah are eternal and unacquired; His acts in the world are the grounds for the gratitude of His creatures.

The al at the opening of al-ḥamd indicates totality (istighrāq): all praise, of every kind, from every creature, in every time, belongs to Allah alone, Blessed and Exalted. Praise offered to other than Allah is ultimately grounded in His bounty, since all beauty, goodness, and perfection in creation are reflections of His attributes.

"You alone we worship, and from You alone we seek help."

The shift from third-person in the earlier verses ("Lord of all the worlds," "The Entirely Merciful, The Especially Merciful," "Master of the Day of Judgment") to second-person address here is a figure of speech called iltifāt (turning): the worshipper has praised Allah and drawn near to Him through that praise, and now turns to address Him directly, as one who has arrived in the presence of the Beloved.

Iyyāka naʿbudu (You alone we worship): worship (ʿibāda) in the complete sense is the submission of the whole self to Allah, encompassing all outward acts and inward states. Wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn (and from You alone we seek help): the slave acknowledges that he is incapable of worship, or of any good, without the enabling grace of Allah. He places worship before seeking help, because the act of worship establishes the relationship; the petition for help flows from the relationship.

"Guide us to the straight path, the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor, not of those who have incurred anger, nor of those who are astray."

The "straight path" (al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of Islam in its completeness: creed, practice, ethics, and purification. It is "straight" because it leads directly to Allah without deviation. The slave asks for guidance to this path in every prayer, in every rakʿa, because guidance is not a single event but a continuous divine gift that must be renewed at every moment.

Those "upon whom You have bestowed favor" are those who believed in what lay beyond their perception (al-ghayb). The ghayb has two kinds: what is hidden from you but known to others, and what is absolutely hidden from all created beings and known only to Allah. The believer accepts both kinds with full conviction based on the testimony of the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace.

The four attributes mentioned at the opening of the sūra for the believers — taqwā, faith in the unseen (al-ghayb), establishment of prayer (iqāmat al-ṣalāh), and spending in charity (al-infāq) — correspond, in the exegetical tradition, to the four rightly guided caliphs. Taqwā corresponds to Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, who excelled in God-consciousness above all. Faith in the unseen corresponds to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who accepted what was reported to him without hesitation. The establishment of prayer corresponds to ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, who was known for his all-night prayers. And spending in charity corresponds to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, about whom, according to the tradition, Q. 2:274 was revealed: "Those who spend their wealth by night and by day, secretly and openly."⁹

In this reading, the opening of Sūrat al-Baqara speaks not only of the general community of believers but of the particular excellences of the four who led that community after the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace. Each caliph embodied in the highest degree one of the four qualities that define the God-fearing. Their example is thus inscribed at the very threshold of the longest sūra in the Qurʾān, as an invitation to the believers of every age to follow in their footsteps.

Having described the elect among His slaves with those qualities and mentioned their ultimate destination, He followed this with the description of His enemies. He dedicated four verses to the characteristics of the believers, two verses to the disbelievers, and thirteen verses to the hypocrites.

Kufr (disbelief) linguistically means covering and concealing. In the terminology of Islamic law, it signifies the denial of something whose arrival from the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, is necessarily known. Al-kāfir (the disbeliever) appears in the Qurʾān in four categories: (1) the disbeliever as the opposite of the believer, as in "Verily those who disbelieve and hinder from the path of Allah" (Q. 4:167); (2) the disbeliever in the sense of one who denies, as in "whoever rejects it — Allah is Self-Sufficient, above all the worlds" (Q. 3:97), meaning whoever denies the obligation of ḥajj; (3) the disbeliever as the opposite of the grateful, as in "Give thanks to Me and do not disbelieve" (Q. 2:152); and (4) the disbeliever in the sense of one who disavows, as in "then on the Day of Resurrection you will disavow one another and curse one another" (Q. 29:25), meaning you will disavow one another.¹

The varieties of kufr are as follows. The kufr of the knowing denier: one who knows the existence of the True, Blessed and Exalted, and yet denies Him by tongue, as in the kufr of Iblīs and the kufr of the Jews: "and when there came to them that which they recognized, they disbelieved in it" (Q. 2:89). The kufr of absolute negation: denying the existence of the Creator entirely, as with most disbelievers. The kufr of one who knows the truth inwardly but does not profess it by tongue and does not practice the religion, as occurred with Abū Ṭālib, who said in verse: "Verily I know that the religion of Muḥammad / is among the best of all the religions of mankind." He uttered this with his tongue and believed it in his heart, yet he did not enter Islām and thus died a disbeliever. And the kufr of hypocrisy (al-nifāq): professing belief by tongue while denying it in the heart.

Kufr, as we established, is the denial of something whose arrival from the Prophet Muḥammad, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, is necessarily known: the oneness of Allah, Blessed and Exalted; the performance of prayers; fasting Ramaḍān; the obligatory nature of zakāh on wealth; ḥajj; and the Resurrection. Whatever is necessarily known to have come from the Prophet, its denial is kufr. As for what cannot be known to have come from the Prophet except through careful investigation and discernment, one who denies it does not become a disbeliever. The distinguishing marks which the scholars have enumerated as acts of kufr — such as wearing the zunnār (the monk's belt) and the ghiyār — are signs of kufr, not kufr in themselves. Whoever is seen wearing the zunnār is declared with certainty to be no believer, because the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, forbade it; yet it carries no self-gratification the way fornication or wine-drinking do. Those are forbidden by the Law, but they contain pleasure for the lower self, and whoever commits them is not a disbeliever because the self overcame him through desire. But wearing the zunnār carries no pleasure whatsoever: whoever wears it has contemptuously dismissed the Islamic religion, and he is a disbeliever. Anyone who meets Allah, Blessed and Exalted, bearing any form of kufr will dwell forever in the Fire: "Verily Allah does not forgive that anything be associated with Him, and He forgives what is less than that for whomever He wills" (Q. 4:48).

The Qurʾān has now established the descriptions of the believers, the disbelievers, and the hypocrites. The Qurʾān began with the "clear" (muḥkam), Sūrat al-Fātiḥa, a perfectly composed sūra, and followed with Sūrat al-Baqara, the first verse of which is "ambiguous" (mutashābih): "Alif. Lām. Mīm." These letters are the weightiest thing for the Arabs, because they understood nothing of what these letters contain. The Qurʾān, in its miraculous nature, is recognized through its eloquence, its rhetorical excellence, and its knowledge of the unseen. The Arabs understood everything that appeared in the Qurʾān until these letters appeared at the openings of certain sūras, and they could discern not the faintest trace of their meaning. These letters are the hardest thing upon them.

Every Arabic word drawn from the roots of the language carries a meaning that matches the nature of the root. For example, the root k-l-m carries in the Arabic language the meaning of force and intensity. This meaning appears in all its derivations: the kalima (word) is so called because it strikes the hearing and the mind of a person until he grasps its meaning; kalam in another sense is the grievous wound, a severe strike. Every word of the Arabs has a derivation and variations. The kalima appears in the Qurʾān in the sense of ʿĪsā, who is called "a word" because he came into being from the command "kun" (Be!), without the agency of a father. And in the usage of the Arabs, they call a full ode a kalima and a sermon a kalima. The Messenger, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, said: "The truest word the poet ever uttered is the word of Labīd: 'Truly, everything besides Allah is vanishing.'" Yet these disconnected letters: the Arabs found in them no trace of meaning. They are therefore the greatest miracle for the Arabs, surpassing all the other miracles of the Qurʾān.⁴⁵

The Sheikh, may Allah be pleased with him, describes the believers and adorns them with the finest qualities: taqwā (God-consciousness), faith in the unseen (al-ghayb), the establishment of prayer (iqāmat al-ṣalāh), and spending from what Allah has provided them.

Prayer is mentioned by Allah in the Qurʾān in a number of verses: "Establish the prayer" (aqīmū al-ṣalāh), meaning not merely to perform a prayer but to establish it, to erect it and perfect it and correct it in every aspect so that it contains no deficiency. Allah commanded the performance of prayer in congregation: "and bow with those who bow" (Q. 2:43). And He commanded that it be performed with full inward submission: "those who are humble in their prayers" (Q. 23:2).

The Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, and those who followed him, may Allah make us and you among them, fulfilled all of these commands entirely: they established the prayer, performed it in congregation and in its proper time, and humbled themselves within it. Allah mentioned them and mentioned their final destination: "Those are the inheritors who shall inherit al-Firdaws; therein they shall abide" (Q. 23:10-11).

He also mentioned those who stand in contrast: those who "belied and turned away" (Q. 75:32) and their recompense: "What has brought you into Saqar? They said: 'We were not among those who prayed'" (Q. 74:42-43). And those who came after and neglected prayer: "Then there followed after them a generation who wasted the prayer and followed desires; they shall encounter perdition" (Q. 19:59). Whenever Allah mentions prayer, He joins it with zakāh, because prayer is the greatest act of bodily worship and spending in charity is the greatest act of worship with wealth; and no one's religion is complete unless he worships with both his body and his wealth.

(And give glad tidings to those who believe), who have affirmed faith in Allah, (and do righteous deeds), from the obligatory acts and the supererogatory acts, (that for them are gardens), orchards with trees and dwellings, (beneath which rivers flow): beneath their trees and their palaces flow the waters. The waters pour forth in Paradise without limit and without machinery; they flow with the inhabitants wherever they are needed.

(Every time they are given provision from its fruits, they say: "This is what we were given before" — and they are brought resemblance thereof): they are offered provision from the fruits of Paradise and say that this resembles what they received before. (And for them therein are purified spouses), purified from menstruation and from every impurity. (And therein they shall abide), dwelling there forever, neither dying nor ever departing from it.¹⁰

O Allah, bestow blessings upon our master Muḥammad, the opener of what had been closed and the seal of what had preceded, the helper of truth through truth, the guide to Your straight path, and upon his family, the true measure of his worth and his immense station.

¹⁰ The tradition concerning the least of the people of Paradise receiving 'eight thousand previously married women (thayyibāt) and four thousand virgins (abkār) and one hundred ḥūrīs' is transmitted in several hadith collections in varying formulations. Al-Qurṭubī discusses such traditions in his commentary on Q. 2:25 (al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān, vol. 1), noting that the ḥūrīs (al-ḥūr al-ʿīn) are described in the Qurʾān as purified spouses (azwāj muṭahhara) and as companions of equal age (atrāb). See also the relevant discussions in Ibn Kathīr's commentary on Q. 44:54 and Q. 56:22-23.
